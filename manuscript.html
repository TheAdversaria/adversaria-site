MS. 014 · The Correspondence of Vane & Ashworth

XIX. What the Cipher Would Not Say

Folio 118–124 · translated from the Ashworth hand · updated Tuesday

Rome had not agreed with the letters. Vane noticed it first in the ink — the way the iron-gall had browned faster than it should, as though the paper itself were impatient to forget what had been written on it. She held the page to the window and the words came up like a bruise, darker where Ashworth had pressed hardest.

He had signed it in the old way, the way he'd stopped using after Trinity: not his name but the mark, three strokes, the private grammar of a man who did not trust his own hand to say a thing plainly and so said it sideways instead.

Original — Latin, marginal hand, folio 121v non quod nolo sed quod timeo — not for want but for fear

She had translated it three times now and each time the second half came out differently. Fear of what, the sentence didn't say. It never did. That was the trouble with Ashworth's Latin — it was a language built for a man who wanted to be found out slowly, in installments, by someone patient enough to keep reading.

Someone, in this case, being her.

· · ·

The library was closed by the time she reached the second letter, but the porter knew her well enough by now to leave the side door on the latch. She had stopped pretending this was research some months ago. Everyone else had stopped pretending to believe her rather earlier than that.

The second letter was shorter, and worse, because it was in English — his own hand, unhidden, uncoded, which meant he had wanted her to understand it the first time, without the labor of translation standing between them like a chaperone.

Ashworth, English, unmarked I keep writing to the edges of the page because I have not yet found the courage to write in the middle of it. You will know what I mean by that, or you won't, and either way I find I no longer have the nerve to say it twice.

She read it standing up, which was a mistake, because it meant there was nowhere to sit down afterward that didn't feel like a decision.

In the margin — his margin, that thin obedient inch he left on every page out of some old schoolboy habit — there was a second note, added later, in a different ink. Smaller. The kind of small that wasn't about space. It was the kind of small that hoped you'd have to lean in to read it, and that leaning in would itself be an answer.
